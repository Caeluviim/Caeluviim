# MAT-PLASMA-2026-08-04-001

## Matter

Compensated plasma extraction in Minnesota.

## Status

Active pre-filing enforcement matter. This record states the relator’s allegations, legal theories, representative purpose, evidentiary predicates, and requested structural relief. It does not convert the matter into an adjudicated judgment.

## Canonical terminology

- commercial plasmapheresis medical practice
- commercial blood-plasma procurement
- compensated plasma extraction
- participant or source-plasma donor when quoting governing law
- statutory enforcement relator under Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a
- representative group of similarly situated Minnesota compensated-plasma participants

## Standing and representative purpose

The standing theory is not limited to an isolated consumer transaction. The relator invokes Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a as the statutory enforcement vehicle for injury caused by conduct governed by the laws identified in subdivision 1. The relator proceeds to obtain relief directed to standardized practices affecting a representative group of similarly situated Minnesota participants.

The representative group is defined functionally by exposure to the same commercial system: recruitment through compensation and repeat-participation incentives; standardized eligibility, consent, risk-disclosure, screening, extraction, testing, deferral, and compensation practices; and the commercial retention and downstream disposition of extracted plasma.

The public-benefit predicate is therefore the correction of practices operating across that group. It is not an afterthought, a substitute for injury, or a request to adjudicate unrelated individual claims. The relator’s injury supplies standing; the standardized and continuing character of the challenged conduct supplies the representative and public enforcement purpose.

## Core transaction model

A commercial plasma center recruits persons through compensation schedules, bonuses, advertising, repeat-participation incentives, eligibility representations, and descriptions of the procedure. The center performs screening, venipuncture, anticoagulant administration, automated extracorporeal separation, return of cellular components, retention of plasma for commercial disposition, and payment.

The transaction is pleaded cumulatively and in the alternative as:

1. compensated procurement in which plasma is transferred for value;
2. a commercial medical-service relationship involving screening, venipuncture, anticoagulant administration, extracorporeal processing, return of blood components, monitoring, and payment;
3. a consumer-facing recruitment and participation program governed by Minnesota statutes prohibiting deceptive, misleading, unfair, or unconscionable commercial practices;
4. a standardized extraction system whose representations, omissions, incentives, and procedures operate upon a representative group and produce public as well as individual consequences.

## Relator allegations

The relator alleges:

- participation at Minnesota commercial plasma centers, including BioLife and Octapharma;
- compensation schedules and new-participant incentives designed to induce repeated procedures;
- a prior low-protein deferral lasting approximately two years;
- repeated venipuncture and automated plasma extraction;
- an Octapharma two-vial sample collected with a 17-gauge venipuncture set and a representation that only one needle type was stocked;
- repeated blood draws described by personnel as occurring twice weekly;
- machine displays presenting extraction totals or weights;
- an observed personal collection value of approximately 820 mL associated with a displayed weight-category value of 142;
- routine observation of displayed totals exceeding 1,000 mL;
- expenditure of time and transportation costs;
- reliance on compensation, eligibility, procedure, safety, testing, and frequency representations;
- cumulative physiological and laboratory consequences, including the pleaded history of low protein;
- absence of adequate longitudinal disclosure and monitoring of cumulative risk;
- standardized practices capable of affecting the representative group in the same manner.

These are relator allegations and observations. They are not recast as claims belonging to unnamed individuals. The representative group supplies the public-benefit and structural-relief scope; the cause of action remains prosecuted by the statutory enforcement relator.

## Federal regulatory frame

Federal regulation governs Source Plasma collection through 21 C.F.R. parts 606, 630, and 640, including donor eligibility and suitability, records, testing, responsible-person and physician functions, collection procedure, frequency, volume, adverse-reaction management, and product disposition.

The regulations are used as operative standards, predicates, notice evidence, falsity evidence, duty evidence, and proof of the regulated medical and commercial character of the conduct. The pleading does not depend on implying a private federal cause of action.

CPT references may be used as corroborative evidence of the recognized medical character of plasmapheresis. They are not the sole predicate for liability.

## Causes of action architecture

### Minnesota Prevention of Consumer Fraud Act and related subdivision 1 laws

Plead through Minn. Stat. §§ 325F.68–325F.70 and Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a. The allegations connect the commercial transaction, intent that participants rely, relator injury, standardized conduct, representative-group exposure, and public-benefit relief.

### False advertising

Apply to identified compensation, safety, eligibility, frequency, testing, procedure, health, and donation-related promotional representations.

### Negligence and professional negligence

Plead the medical and operational duties arising from screening, venipuncture, anticoagulant use, extracorporeal processing, collection volume, repeat frequency, testing, monitoring, and adverse-reaction management. Procedural affidavit requirements are filing mechanics, not grounds to erase the substantive theory.

### Lack of informed consent

Plead standardized omission or minimization of cumulative risks, the disclosures actually supplied, the materiality of the omitted information, decision causation, and resulting injury.

### Unjust enrichment and restitution

Plead as alternative relief tied to the procurement and retention of plasma through the challenged practices. Downstream value evidence supports the scale and economic context of the enterprise and the design of structural relief; it is not automatically equated with individual compensatory damages.

### Declaratory and structural equitable relief

Seek declarations and injunctions directed to standardized recruitment, consent, testing, collection-volume, monitoring, compensation, and risk-disclosure practices affecting the representative group.

## Structural-relief architecture

The requested Minnesota Structural Relief Pool of not less than $3.6 billion is preserved as the relator’s proposed remedial architecture. Its purpose is not to assign industry revenue as personal damages. Its purpose is to fund representative-group remediation, notice, independent medical assessment, longitudinal monitoring, restitution administration, disclosure correction, compliance auditing, and institutional reform proportionate to the alleged statewide extraction system.

The amount must be supported through admissible economic proof, statewide transaction evidence, downstream-value evidence, duration, participant count, remedial cost, and equitable administration design. The demand is preserved rather than deleted or relabeled as merely speculative.

## Drafting rule

No future repository artifact may:

1. replace “statutory enforcement relator” with “ordinary private plaintiff” as the canonical project theory;
2. erase the representative group for whose benefit standing and public enforcement are invoked;
3. characterize representative relief as adjudication of unnamed individuals’ personal claims;
4. delete the structural-relief demand merely because final quantum proof will be developed through discovery and expert evidence;
5. call defendant-controlled corroborating records an evidentiary gap;
6. reduce the case to a semantic dispute about the word “donation” rather than plead the full commercial, medical, economic, and institutional conduct.
