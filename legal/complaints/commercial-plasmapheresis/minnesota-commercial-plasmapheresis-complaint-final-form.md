# COMPLAINT FOR DECLARATORY, INJUNCTIVE, RESTITUTIONARY, AND MONETARY RELIEF

## JURY TRIAL DEMANDED

Plaintiff alleges as follows:

## INTRODUCTION

1. This action challenges standardized public-facing representations, omissions, incentive programs, and participation practices used to recruit Minnesota residents into repeated compensated plasma-extraction procedures.

2. Defendants acquire commercially valuable Source Plasma through automated plasmapheresis while publicly and contractually describing participants principally as “donors” engaged in “donation.” Defendants also provide monetary compensation, escalating bonus schedules, repeat-participation incentives, and promotional awards tied to completion of multiple procedures.

3. Plaintiff does not allege that compensation by itself is unlawful or that use of the word “donation,” standing alone, establishes liability. The challenged practice is the total commercial presentation: altruistic or charitable framing combined with payment-based recruitment, standardized omissions, and incomplete disclosure of the bilateral commercial exchange, invasive procedure, extraction volume, cumulative participation history, monitoring limits, deferral consequences, and downstream commercial use of the acquired plasma.

4. Federal regulation defines plasmapheresis as a procedure in which blood is removed, plasma is separated from formed elements, and at least the red blood cells are returned to the participant. 21 C.F.R. § 640.65(a).

5. Federal regulations require, among other things, medical-history and physical-examination review, informed consent, pre-procedure total-protein testing, periodic laboratory testing, responsible-physician review, specified deferral rules, and collection-frequency limits. See 21 C.F.R. §§ 630.15(b), 640.65(b).

6. Plaintiff cites those federal requirements as regulatory context bearing on materiality, public policy, procedure characterization, and the adequacy of Defendants’ representations and disclosures. Plaintiff does not plead those regulations as independent private federal causes of action.

7. Plaintiff seeks relief under the Minnesota False Statement in Advertisement Act, Minn. Stat. § 325F.67; the Minnesota Prevention of Consumer Fraud Act, Minn. Stat. §§ 325F.68–.70; the Minnesota Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act, Minn. Stat. §§ 325D.43–.45; the private-remedies provision of Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a; and the Minnesota Uniform Declaratory Judgments Act, Minn. Stat. ch. 555.

## FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

### A. Commercial acquisition through automated plasmapheresis

21. Defendants acquire Source Plasma for downstream commercial use, processing, manufacture, distribution, research, or sale.

22. The acquisition occurs through automated plasmapheresis, an invasive procedure involving venipuncture, extracorporeal circulation, separation of plasma from cellular components, anticoagulant use, retention of plasma, and return of cellular components.

23. Federal regulation requires the volume collected through automated plasmapheresis to be consistent with volumes specifically approved by the FDA and limits collection to no less than two days apart and no more than twice in seven days. 21 C.F.R. § 640.65(b)(8).

24. Federal regulation requires Defendants to weigh the participant at each procedure and determine total plasma protein before each plasmapheresis procedure. 21 C.F.R. § 630.15(b)(3)–(4).

25. Federal regulation also requires periodic laboratory testing and responsible-physician review of accumulated laboratory data and collection records. 21 C.F.R. § 640.65(b)(1)–(2).

26. These requirements confirm that the transaction involves a regulated, repeated procedure and longitudinal eligibility information, not merely the passive transfer of an ordinary consumer good.

### B. Public recruitment and compensation architecture

27. Defendants recruit members of the public through websites, mobile applications, text or email messages, in-center signs, referral programs, promotional materials, and other advertisements.

28. Defendants’ recruitment materials repeatedly use “donate,” “donation,” and “donor” as the dominant transaction language.

29. Defendants simultaneously offer monetary compensation and may structure compensation through different amounts for first and later weekly procedures, new-participant bonuses, completion bonuses, referral awards, loyalty programs, lotteries, or other promotional incentives.

30. Based on Plaintiff’s experience, compensation examples included approximately **$45 for one procedure and $75 for another procedure in the same week**, approximately **$120 per week**, and new-participant promotions advertised at approximately **$800 for completion of eight procedures**, subject to verification from the applicable advertisements and payment records.

31. Plaintiff also observed or received promotional references to prizes or lottery-style awards of **$500 or more**, subject to documentary verification.

32. The compensation architecture encourages participants to complete multiple invasive procedures within compressed time periods and makes deferral, missed appointments, or inability to complete a sequence economically consequential.

33. Plaintiff alleges that the dominant “donation” framing and compensation architecture do not provide equally prominent, plain-language disclosure that the participant is entering a bilateral commercial plasma-procurement transaction in which Defendants obtain a commercially valuable biological product.

34. Plaintiff further alleges that the commercial value and downstream use of the acquired plasma are not disclosed with prominence comparable to the compensation and “donation” messaging.

### C. Plaintiff’s participation and observations

35. Plaintiff participated at **[CENTER NAME AND ADDRESS]** on or about **[DATES OR DATE RANGE]** and at **[ADDITIONAL CENTER NAME AND ADDRESS]** on or about **[DATES OR DATE RANGE]**.

36. Plaintiff’s total time cost for travel, intake, screening, waiting, procedure completion, and return travel was frequently approximately four to six hours per participation day.

37. Plaintiff incurred transportation expense of approximately **$8 per participation day**, subject to confirmation through fare or travel records.

38. Plaintiff observed machine-displayed procedure values that appeared to reflect an extraction amount of approximately **820 mL** for Plaintiff’s weight category and observed displayed values exceeding **1,000 mL** for some other participants.

39. Plaintiff does not allege that a displayed number alone proves an unlawful collection volume. Defendants’ procedure logs, device configuration, FDA-approved collection protocol, participant weight, anticoagulant accounting, and actual retained-plasma volume are required to determine what each displayed value represented.

40. At an Octapharma-branded center, Plaintiff observed use of an approximately 17-gauge venipuncture needle for collection of two sample vials and was informed or understood that the center stocked a single needle type for that purpose, subject to verification through supply and procedure records.

41. Plaintiff observed or was told that full-panel or multi-vial blood sampling occurred at repeated participations after initial clearance, potentially as often as twice weekly, subject to verification through laboratory orders, specimen logs, procedure records, and explanations provided to Plaintiff.

42. Plaintiff does not allege that the federal four-month periodic-testing rule prohibits more frequent testing. Plaintiff alleges that the purpose, frequency, specimen volume, use, retention, and consequences of repeated blood sampling were not adequately explained in a manner sufficient to permit informed evaluation of the complete participation burden.

43. Plaintiff experienced a low-protein deferral lasting approximately two years, subject to verification through Defendants’ deferral records and laboratory results.

44. Federal regulations require pre-procedure total-protein levels between 6.0 and 9.0 grams per deciliter and require deferral and physician approval for reinstatement under specified circumstances. 21 C.F.R. §§ 630.15(b)(4), 640.65(b)(2)(i).

45. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants did not provide a complete, readily usable longitudinal record showing procedure dates, retained-plasma volumes, test results, deferral bases, cumulative participation frequency, red-cell loss events, and responsible-physician determinations.

### D. Alleged disclosure deficiencies

46. Federal regulation requires informed consent at the first procedure and at intervals no longer than one year and requires explanation of procedure risks and hazards in a manner giving the participant a clear opportunity to refuse. 21 C.F.R. § 630.15(b)(2).

47. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ standardized consent and recruitment process did not adequately integrate the annual consent event with the actual high-frequency, compensation-driven pattern of repeated procedures.

48. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants did not clearly and prominently disclose, before participation:

   a. that the transaction is compensated commercial procurement of Source Plasma;

   b. that the acquired plasma is retained for downstream commercial use, processing, manufacture, distribution, research, or sale;

   c. the actual retained-plasma volume for each procedure and the meaning of machine-displayed values;

   d. the distinction between whole-blood volume, plasma volume, anticoagulant volume, extracorporeal volume, and returned cellular components;

   e. the cumulative procedure history used to assess continued eligibility;

   f. the scope and limits of longitudinal monitoring;

   g. the practical and economic consequences of low-protein or other deferral during an incentive sequence;

   h. the full time and transportation burden reasonably associated with earning advertised compensation;

   i. the purpose and frequency of repeated specimen collection; and

   j. material cumulative risks or uncertainties associated with repeated participation at the frequency promoted.

49. Plaintiff alleges that those omitted or obscured facts would matter to a reasonable person deciding whether, how often, and under what compensation terms to participate.

50. Defendants possess or control records necessary to test these allegations, including advertisements, compensation schedules, consent forms, procedure logs, device records, extraction-volume records, laboratory records, physician-review records, deferral records, adverse-event records, training records, credential records, referral records, and promotional-award materials.

### E. Causation and injury

51. Plaintiff encountered and relied on the overall standardized presentation of the program, including “donation” terminology, compensation offers, repeat-participation incentives, screening representations, consent materials, and in-center explanations.

52. Plaintiff participated repeatedly, furnished plasma, surrendered time, incurred transportation expense, underwent invasive procedures, and organized conduct around Defendants’ compensation and eligibility structure.

53. Had Defendants clearly disclosed the commercial character, complete procedure burden, actual retained volume, cumulative participation information, monitoring limits, and material deferral consequences, Plaintiff would have evaluated participation, frequency, center selection, and compensation differently.

54. Defendants’ conduct caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s alleged economic, informational, procedural, and other legally cognizable injuries.

55. The same standardized practices remain capable of affecting other current and prospective Minnesota participants.

## COUNT I

### FALSE STATEMENT IN ADVERTISEMENT
### Minn. Stat. § 325F.67, enforced through Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a

56. Plaintiff incorporates paragraphs 1–55.

57. Defendants made, published, disseminated, circulated, or placed before the Minnesota public advertisements intended to induce participation in compensated plasma extraction and to induce members of the public to enter obligations relating to Defendants’ program.

58. The advertised program, procedure, compensation opportunity, and commercial plasma-procurement transaction constitute a service, thing offered to the public, or matter offered for use, participation, acquisition, or sale within Minn. Stat. § 325F.67.

59. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ advertisements contained materially untrue, deceptive, or misleading assertions or presentations, including by:

   a. presenting compensated commercial plasma procurement principally as altruistic “donation” without equally prominent commercial characterization;

   b. emphasizing gross compensation or bonus amounts without adequate disclosure of the time, transportation, eligibility, completion, deferral, and repeated-procedure conditions materially affecting realization of those amounts;

   c. omitting or obscuring material facts concerning procedure volume, cumulative participation history, monitoring limits, repeated specimen collection, and downstream commercial use; and

   d. creating a misleading overall impression about the nature, burden, benefits, and risks of repeated compensated plasmapheresis.

60. Defendants intended that members of the public act on the advertisements by enrolling, presenting for procedures, completing repeated procedures, furnishing plasma, and complying with incentive conditions.

61. Plaintiff acted in response to the standardized advertising and program presentation and suffered injury causally connected to the alleged deception.

62. Enforcement will benefit the public because the challenged advertisements and practices are directed to the public at large and remain capable of influencing current and prospective Minnesota participants.

63. Plaintiff is entitled to damages, investigation costs, costs and disbursements, reasonable attorney fees where authorized, and equitable relief under Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a.

## COUNT II

### PREVENTION OF CONSUMER FRAUD ACT
### Minn. Stat. §§ 325F.68–.70, enforced through Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a

64. Plaintiff incorporates paragraphs 1–63.

65. Minn. Stat. § 325F.69, subd. 1 prohibits fraud, unfair or unconscionable practices, false pretenses, false promises, misrepresentations, misleading statements, and deceptive practices intended to induce reliance in connection with the sale of merchandise.

66. “Merchandise” includes services and intangibles. Minn. Stat. § 325F.68, subd. 2.

67. Plaintiff pleads in the alternative that Defendants offered and provided a plasmapheresis participation service and associated screening, procedure, records, eligibility, and compensation program for consideration in a bilateral commercial exchange.

68. The consideration included Plaintiff’s plasma, time, repeated attendance, compliance with screening and procedure requirements, personal and medical information, and performance of incentive conditions, in exchange for Defendants’ procedure services, eligibility administration, and compensation.

69. Plaintiff was a direct recipient and consumer of the procedure and program services even though Defendants also compensated Plaintiff for the plasma obtained.

70. Defendants used misleading statements, deceptive practices, material omissions, and unfair or unconscionable practices in connection with that transaction, including the conduct alleged in paragraphs 27–50.

71. The challenged practices allegedly offend public policy reflected in Minnesota consumer-protection law and federal plasmapheresis regulations, are unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous, and are substantially injurious to participants within Minn. Stat. § 325F.69, subd. 8.

72. Defendants intended that Plaintiff and other members of the public rely on the standardized presentation by enrolling, repeatedly participating, furnishing plasma, and completing incentive sequences.

73. Plaintiff alleges a causal nexus between the challenged conduct and Plaintiff’s injury. Plaintiff does not rely solely on an allegation of abstract deception.

74. The action serves a public benefit because it challenges standardized public-facing practices and seeks prospective relief for current and future Minnesota participants.

75. Plaintiff is entitled to damages, costs and disbursements, investigation costs, reasonable attorney fees where authorized, and equitable relief under Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a.

76. If the Court determines that the bilateral transaction is not sufficiently connected to a sale of merchandise under Minn. Stat. § 325F.69, Plaintiff pleads Counts I, III, and IV independently and in the alternative.

## COUNT III

### UNIFORM DECEPTIVE TRADE PRACTICES ACT
### Minn. Stat. §§ 325D.44–.45

77. Plaintiff incorporates paragraphs 1–76.

78. In the course of business, Defendants caused a likelihood of confusion or misunderstanding concerning the nature, characteristics, benefits, quantities, commercial purpose, and material conditions of their compensated plasmapheresis services and participation program.

79. Defendants represented or created the impression that the offered program possessed characteristics, benefits, or a transaction character materially different from the complete commercial and procedural reality alleged above.

80. Defendants engaged in unfair or unconscionable acts or practices and other conduct creating a likelihood of confusion or misunderstanding within Minn. Stat. § 325D.44, subd. 1(5), (13), and (14).

81. Actual confusion is not required under Minn. Stat. § 325D.44, subd. 2.

82. Plaintiff and members of the public are likely to be damaged by continued use of the challenged standardized representations and practices.

83. Plaintiff is entitled to injunctive relief under Minn. Stat. § 325D.45, subd. 1, costs as allowed by law, and attorney fees if the statutory willfulness standard is proved.

## COUNT IV

### DECLARATORY JUDGMENT
### Minn. Stat. ch. 555

84. Plaintiff incorporates paragraphs 1–83.

85. An actual and justiciable controversy exists concerning the legality of Defendants’ standardized advertisements, disclosures, transaction characterization, compensation presentation, and repeated-participation practices.

86. Plaintiff contends that the challenged conduct is deceptive, misleading, unfair, or unconscionable under Minnesota law. Defendants have acted and continue to act as though their current standardized practices are lawful and sufficient.

87. A declaration will terminate uncertainty concerning the parties’ legal rights and obligations and will support effective prospective relief.

88. Plaintiff seeks declarations that, to the extent proved:

   a. Defendants’ challenged advertisements and standardized program presentation violate Minn. Stat. § 325F.67;

   b. Defendants’ challenged conduct violates Minn. Stat. § 325F.69;

   c. Defendants’ challenged conduct constitutes deceptive trade practices under Minn. Stat. § 325D.44;

   d. compensated automated plasmapheresis is a bilateral commercial plasma-procurement transaction involving a regulated invasive procedure and may not be presented in a materially misleading manner as though it were exclusively charitable or altruistic; and

   e. corrective disclosures and related equitable measures are required to prevent continuing deception.

89. Plaintiff does not request a declaration that rewrites federal regulation, creates a private federal cause of action, or establishes that compensation or plasmapheresis is categorically unlawful.

## PRAYER FOR RELIEF

Plaintiff requests judgment as follows:

A. Declaring that Defendants violated the Minnesota statutes identified above to the extent established by the evidence;

B. Enjoining Defendants from using materially misleading “donation” framing without clear and comparably prominent disclosure that the participant undergoes compensated commercial plasma extraction and that the acquired plasma is retained for downstream commercial use;

C. Requiring corrective disclosures sufficient to prevent deception, including disclosure of the procedure’s commercial character, material compensation conditions, actual retained-plasma volume, meaning of machine-displayed values, procedure frequency, material testing and deferral rules, cumulative participation history, monitoring scope, and material risks or uncertainties established by competent evidence;

D. Requiring Defendants to preserve and, where legally appropriate, provide participants reasonable access to procedure dates, retained-plasma volumes, laboratory results, deferral bases, red-cell loss events, and responsible-physician review information;

E. Awarding actual, consequential, and other damages authorized by law and proved at trial;

F. Awarding restitution, disgorgement, or other restorationary relief to the extent legally available and supported by the evidence;

G. Awarding costs and disbursements, including investigation costs and reasonable attorney fees under Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a, and any other applicable authority;

H. Awarding declaratory and injunctive relief under Minn. Stat. ch. 555 and Minn. Stat. § 325D.45;

I. Reserving the amount and structure of any prospective remediation or structural-relief fund until supported by admissible economic, participation, revenue, and injury evidence; and

J. Granting such other relief as the Court deems just and equitable.
