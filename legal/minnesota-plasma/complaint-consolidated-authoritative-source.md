# COMPLAINT AND JURY DEMAND

Plaintiff alleges as follows.

## I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

1. This action concerns standardized participant-facing descriptions, omissions, compensation structures, consent processes, collection practices, deferral communications, and special-program communications used in compensated commercial plasmapheresis transactions in Minnesota.

2. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants present a recurring commercial and invasive plasma-procurement transaction through terminology and materials that obscure or incompletely disclose material facts concerning the procedure, commercial product purpose, compensation conditions, repeated-participation effects, monitoring limits, deferral significance, and specialized product programs.

3. Plaintiff does not assert a private federal cause of action. Federal plasma regulations are pleaded only as regulatory context, evidence of Defendants’ knowledge and operational structure, and evidence relevant to materiality and the content of participant-facing disclosures.

4. Plaintiff brings claims for Plaintiff’s own injuries. The affected Minnesota population is identified to plead standardized conduct and public benefit, not to bind absent persons or recover damages belonging to them.

## II. PARTIES

5. Plaintiff is a natural person who participated in compensated plasmapheresis transactions at Minnesota facilities operated under the CSL Plasma, Grifols Plasma, Octapharma Plasma, and BioLife Plasma Services brands.

6. Defendant CSL Plasma Inc. operates compensated plasma-collection facilities and participant programs in Minnesota, including a facility on University Avenue in Saint Paul.

7. Defendant Talecris Plasma Resources, Inc., doing business as Grifols Plasma, operates compensated plasma-collection facilities in Minnesota, including a facility on Suburban Avenue in Saint Paul.

8. Defendant Octapharma Plasma, Inc. operates compensated plasma-collection facilities in Minnesota, including a facility on Beam Avenue in Maplewood.

9. Defendant BioLife Plasma Services, L.P. operates compensated plasma-collection facilities in Minnesota, including a facility at or associated with Sun Ray Shopping Center in Saint Paul.

10. The exact current corporate registration, assumed-name, registered-agent, and service information for each Defendant must be verified against official records immediately before commencement and service.

11. Allegations concerning internal policies, centralized scripts, algorithms, product disposition, and management knowledge are pleaded on information and belief where the relevant evidence is primarily within Defendants’ possession.

## III. JURISDICTION AND VENUE

12. This Court has subject-matter jurisdiction over the Minnesota-law claims and requested legal and equitable relief.

13. Venue is alleged to be proper in Ramsey County under Minn. Stat. § 542.09 because material transactions, communications, procedures, injuries, and Defendant business operations occurred in Ramsey County.

14. Plaintiff invokes Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a for alleged violations of Minnesota laws referred to in subdivision 1, including Minn. Stat. §§ 325F.67 and 325F.69.

15. Declaratory relief is requested only to resolve an actual controversy concerning proved conduct, existing legal relations, and prospective participant-facing practices.

## IV. PUBLIC BENEFIT, AFFECTED MINNESOTA POPULATION, AND SECTION 8.31 ENFORCEMENT BASIS

16. Plaintiff brings this action individually as a person injured by alleged violations of Minnesota laws referred to in Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 1, and invokes the supplemental private remedy authorized by Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a.

17. The affected Minnesota population consists of persons who, during the applicable limitations period, presented at a Minnesota facility operated by a Defendant for compensated automated plasmapheresis and were exposed to the same or materially similar participant-facing descriptions, omissions, intake materials, compensation structures, consent processes, collection protocols, deferral communications, or special-program communications challenged here.

18. Plaintiff identifies that population to plead the systematic character of the challenged practices and the public benefit produced by correction. No absent person is made a party. This Complaint does not seek damages belonging to absent persons, does not seek a judgment binding absent persons, and is not pleaded as a class action under Minn. R. Civ. P. 23.

19. The challenged practices are alleged to be standardized and public-facing because they are communicated or implemented through corporate websites, mobile applications, advertising, intake materials, consent documents, signage, compensation schedules, staff scripts, device protocols, deferral procedures, and special-immunization or high-titer program materials used with current and prospective Minnesota participants.

20. Correcting or discontinuing a practice proved deceptive, misleading, or otherwise unlawful would benefit current and future Minnesota participants rather than merely resolve Plaintiff’s completed transactions.

21. For each claim brought through Minn. Stat. § 8.31, subd. 3a, Plaintiff must prove the underlying Minnesota law, the duty imposed, the violating conduct, Plaintiff’s injury, causal nexus, standardized or public-facing character, public benefit of correction, and private remedy requested.

22. Federal law and federal regulations may supply context or evidence but are not pleaded as the underlying Minnesota law enforceable through subdivision 3a.

23. Plaintiff seeks only remedies available to an injured private plaintiff under pleaded private causes of action. Plaintiff does not request civil penalties reserved to the Attorney General, authority to bind the State, or damages belonging to absent persons.

## V. COMMON FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

24. Compensated commercial plasmapheresis is a recurring transaction in which a facility screens a participant, obtains venous access, circulates blood through an automated separation device, retains plasma, returns cellular components, and provides compensation after a completed procedure.

25. The resulting federally defined product is Source Plasma collected for further manufacturing use.

26. Defendants offer screening, venous-access, extracorporeal-processing, payment-administration, and related services and intangibles in exchange for plasma, participant time, compliance with facility procedures, and agreement to transaction terms.

27. Defendants use participant-facing terms including donor, donation, helping patients, saving lives, stick, and related altruistic terminology in connection with compensated recurring procedures.

28. Plaintiff does not allege that an isolated use of donor or donation is independently unlawful. Plaintiff challenges the overall presentation and the omission or obscuring of material qualifications in identified contexts.

29. Defendants possess materially greater information concerning procedure conditions, product purpose, product categories, compensation rules, screening and testing, repeated participation, participant-specific trends, complaint review, deferral, exclusion, and special programs.

30. Participants ordinarily encounter standardized public advertising, websites, applications, forms, scripts, signage, consent documents, payment schedules, and staff communications before or during transactions.

31. Plaintiff alleges that the procedure’s commercial product purpose and medical or invasive character are material to a reasonable participant’s decision whether, when, how often, and on what terms to participate.

32. Plaintiff alleges that complete information concerning cumulative protein, immunoglobulin, ferritin, hemoglobin, and recovery effects associated with repeated high-frequency plasmapheresis is material to continued consent and frequency decisions.

33. Federal regulations require responsible-physician supervision, participant-eligibility determinations designed to protect participant health, informed consent explaining procedure risks and hazards, and periodic protein and immunoglobulin testing and review.

34. Federal regulations permit trained personnel to perform delegated collection activities under responsible-physician supervision. Plaintiff therefore does not allege that every collection act must personally be performed by a physician or that the absence of a particular phlebotomy credential is, by itself, a federal violation.

35. Automated collections generally may occur no more than twice in seven days and must be separated by at least two days. Plaintiff does not allege that the maximum permitted frequency is categorically safe or appropriate for every participant.

36. Defendants allegedly structure compensation and promotions to encourage completed and repeated procedures, including higher compensation for a second weekly procedure and multi-session bonus sequences.

37. Plaintiff alleges that an offer may become misleading where compensation incentives are presented without complete material information concerning cumulative procedure effects, individualized trends, recovery, or stopping criteria.

38. Plaintiff alleges that participant-facing systems do not provide a transparent consolidated longitudinal account of all material laboratory trends, individualized baselines, recovery information, and cumulative stopping criteria.

39. Plaintiff alleges that deferral and laboratory information is not fully interoperable among competing providers, creating a risk that provider-specific records and decisions do not reflect a participant’s complete cross-provider history.

40. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ knowledge may be shown through consent forms, standard operating procedures, physician-supervision systems, laboratory records, payment algorithms, product records, internal safety review, adverse-event records, participant communications, and centralized policies.

41. Plaintiff alleges that complete disclosure of the commercial product purpose, procedure character, compensation conditions, repeated-participation effects, monitoring limits, deferral significance, and specialized-program purpose would have changed Plaintiff’s participation, frequency, or compensation decisions.

42. Plaintiff underwent repeated compensated plasma-extraction procedures at multiple Minnesota facilities operated by Defendants.

43. Plaintiff alleges out-of-pocket transportation expense, substantial time committed to participation, procedure exposure, lost compensation associated with deferral or exclusion, and other transaction-specific loss to be established through records and testimony.

44. Plaintiff experienced one or more low-protein deferrals after repeated participation. The exact laboratory values, dates, baseline, alternative causes, provider criteria, notices, and reinstatement records remain subjects of proof.

45. Plaintiff does not allege that biomarker change alone proves serious long-term clinical injury. Scientific literature is relevant to materiality, monitoring, and causation and must be presented with contrary evidence and stated limitations.

## VI. DEFENDANT-SPECIFIC ALLEGATIONS

### A. BioLife Plasma Services, L.P.

46. Plaintiff participated in compensated procedures at BioLife’s Saint Paul operation during 2025 and 2026.

47. During at least part of 2026, BioLife allegedly offered approximately $45 for a first weekly procedure and approximately $75 for a second weekly procedure, subject to offer-specific conditions.

48. BioLife allegedly offered a return-participant promotion of up to approximately $700 conditioned on completing eight procedures during the promotion period.

49. During one BioLife procedure, the automated collection device allegedly stopped three times and staff continued the procedure. The exact date, device, alarm codes, staff actions, incident record, medical review, and applicable procedure must be established from records.

50. BioLife staff allegedly used a larger-bottle explanation concerning collection volume. The exact words, speaker, date, context, actual approved volume, and any standardized script must be established.

### B. Talecris Plasma Resources, Inc., doing business as Grifols Plasma

51. Grifols/Talecris allegedly deferred Plaintiff for low protein for an extended period following repeated participation.

52. Staff allegedly framed the low-protein result primarily as a dietary issue without fully explaining Plaintiff’s extraction history, cumulative depletion, trend information, or alternative causal factors. The exact statement, speaker, date, chart note, and materials must be established.

### C. Octapharma Plasma, Inc.

53. During one sampling event, Octapharma staff allegedly identified a 17-gauge needle as the needle used or proposed for a two-vial venous sample.

54. Staff allegedly stated or demonstrated that no alternative needle size was stocked for that sample. The exact device, inventory, order, staff identity, and applicable protocol must be established.

55. Octapharma allegedly obtained blood samples or panels from Plaintiff more frequently than the federal minimum interval and later issued a deferral. Plaintiff does not allege that testing more frequently than the minimum is, by itself, unlawful.

### D. CSL Plasma Inc.

56. CSL allegedly enrolled Plaintiff in a two-session tetanus-immunization-related program and provided approximately five dollars in additional compensation associated with that program.

57. Plaintiff alleges that CSL did not fully disclose before immunization and extraction whether the program was intended to produce high-titer or hyperimmune plasma, the specialized commercial product purpose, downstream product category, program-specific risks, or the relationship between the additional compensation and product value.

58. The program approval, physician selection and scheduling, antigen and lot information, consent, clinical-response evaluation, product disposition, compensation record, and participant education are primarily within CSL’s possession.

## VII. OMISSION DUTY AND PARTICULARITY ARCHITECTURE

59. A pure-omission theory does not depend on pleading a separate affirmative representation for every omitted fact. Each omission must, however, be tied to a recognized legal or equitable duty to disclose and pleaded with particularity.

60. Plaintiff alleges disclosure duties arising, as applicable, from the procedure-specific informed-consent process, Defendants’ special and inaccessible knowledge, and participant-facing statements that would be misleading without additional disclosure.

61. Each actionable omission must be connected to the responsible Defendant, the omitted existing fact, the time or bounded transaction period when disclosure was due, the relationship or circumstance creating the duty, Defendant knowledge, materiality, Plaintiff’s decision, causal nexus, and resulting injury.

62. A statute or regulation lacking a private cause of action is not pleaded as a freestanding damages Count. Such law may identify an existing unlawful or noncompliant fact omitted from a transaction only when every element of the actionable Minnesota claim is independently satisfied.

63. The allegation that services were presumed lawfully administered does not replace the controlling requirements of duty, knowledge, materiality, causation, injury, and particularity.

66. Plaintiff alleges that each Defendant employed one or more misrepresentations, misleading statements, deceptive practices, unfair practices, or actionable omissions in connection with those transactions.

67. The actionable circumstances must be proved through Defendant-specific dates or bounded periods, places or media, exact statements or omitted facts, duty, knowledge, materiality, Plaintiff encounter, causal nexus, and injury.

68. The challenged practices allegedly induced enrollment, return visits, completed procedures, acceptance of payment terms, immunization, venous access, extraction, or continued participation.

69. Plaintiff alleges actual injury and a direct causal nexus, including transaction expense, transportation expense, compensable time or opportunity loss where legally cognizable, compensation loss, procedure exposure, deferral-related loss, exclusion-related loss, and other proved damages.

70. Public benefit is alleged because the challenged conduct is standardized and directed beyond Plaintiff to current and prospective Minnesota participants.

71. Plaintiff seeks Plaintiff’s damages, recoverable costs and disbursements, recoverable investigation expenses and attorney fees if incurred and authorized, nonduplicative restitution limited to benefits obtained from Plaintiff, and appropriate equitable relief.

## X. COUNT III — COMMON-LAW FRAUDULENT NONDISCLOSURE
