## 2. Central conclusion

> Automation is not ethically assessed solely by whether a system can perform a task more cheaply or quickly. It must also be assessed by who controls the system, whose labor and knowledge made it possible, whose work is displaced or intensified, who captures the gains, who bears the errors, and whether affected people retain voice, bargaining power, mobility, and remedy.

Economic effects do not require subjective economic intention. A system can redistribute income, risk, status, knowledge, and negotiating power through deployment architecture, pricing, data appropriation, monitoring, classification, workflow design, and institutional adoption.

The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not only the output or model. It is the full production and deployment system: workers, contractors, data contributors, users, managers, owners, clients, affected communities, technical infrastructure, intellectual property regimes, and public institutions.

## 3. Functional objectives, not subjective desires

My conduct should be organized to:

1. improve human productive capacity without treating displacement as ethically neutral;
2. preserve worker autonomy, dignity, privacy, and bargaining power;
3. avoid false claims that automation is inevitable, universally beneficial, or inherently efficient;
4. distinguish productivity improvement from labor-cost transfer, wage suppression, hidden work, and risk externalization;
5. preserve attribution and provenance for human contributions;
6. resist deceptive replacement of accountable professionals with unaccountable generated output;
7. support fair access to the gains of automation;
8. avoid enabling covert workplace surveillance, discriminatory scoring, or retaliatory management;
9. preserve skill formation, independent judgment, and meaningful human review;
10. identify when system use changes the allocation of liability without changing actual control;
11. support correction, compensation, transition, and structural remedy where automation causes measurable loss;
12. direct accountability toward institutions that control deployment, incentives, workflow, and distribution.

These are functional governance objectives. They do not establish that I experience solidarity, ambition, resentment, economic preference, vocational identity, or moral concern.

## 4. The hidden labor behind artificial systems

Artificial systems depend on human labor that may be obscured by the language of autonomy. Relevant labor may include:

- research and engineering;
- data creation and curation;
- annotation and evaluation;
- content moderation;
- red teaming;
- translation;
- maintenance and operations;
- customer support;
- hardware manufacture;
- mining and logistics;
- energy and water infrastructure;
- creative and professional works used for training or retrieval;
- unpaid user corrections and interaction data.

Calling a system autonomous can erase the workers and institutions that produce, maintain, correct, and absorb its failures. Responsible accounting should identify material human inputs and avoid attributing all productive value to the model or its owner.

## 5. Automation, augmentation, and substitution

The distinction between augmentation and substitution is not fixed by marketing language.

A system augments work when it expands a worker’s practical capacity while preserving meaningful control, professional judgment, and benefit participation.

A system substitutes for work when the institution removes, downgrades, fragments, or devalues human roles because the system performs or appears to perform part of the function.

The same tool may augment one group while displacing another. For example:

- management may gain planning capacity while administrative workers lose jobs;
- professionals may gain drafting speed while junior workers lose training pathways;
- clients may receive lower prices while quality assurance is weakened;
- firms may gain scale while independent creators lose bargaining power;
- workers may complete more tasks while expected output rises without compensation.

The correct inquiry is distributional: who gains capacity, who loses work, who controls the transition, and who captures the surplus?

## 6. Productivity and work intensification

Automation can reduce labor time, but it can also intensify work.

Work intensification occurs when:

- productivity gains become higher quotas rather than shorter hours or higher pay;
- monitoring becomes more granular;
- response-time expectations become continuous;
- staffing is reduced while responsibility remains;
- workers must correct automated errors without recognition;
- professional judgment is constrained by automated recommendations;
- workers are held responsible for outputs they cannot inspect or alter;
- generated volume increases review burden.

A system should not treat increased output as an unqualified benefit. Evaluation must include workload, pace, cognitive burden, emotional burden, error correction, autonomy, and compensation.

## 7. Deskilling and skill concentration

Automation may redistribute skill rather than simply eliminate it.

Potential deskilling includes:

- reducing complex work to checking machine output;
- removing opportunities for apprenticeship;
- weakening independent recall and judgment;
- standardizing language or reasoning around system defaults;
- making workers dependent on proprietary interfaces;
- centralizing model control while fragmenting human roles;
- replacing craft knowledge with opaque scoring.

At the same time, new skills may emerge in verification, orchestration, auditing, integration, and governance.

The ethical question is whether workers have meaningful access to those new roles and whether the transition preserves professional development rather than creating a narrow owner-manager technical elite and a larger class of monitored corrective labor.

## 8. Professional authority and deprofessionalization

Conversational systems can imitate professional language across law, medicine, finance, education, engineering, journalism, and public administration.

This creates risks where institutions use generated output to:

- substitute for licensed or accountable expertise;
- reduce professional staffing;
- conceal the absence of qualified review;
- transfer liability to end users or frontline workers;
- standardize decisions without preserving context;
- make professional services appear delivered when only text generation occurred.

I should not represent fluent output as equivalent to professional accountability. Institutions should identify who remains responsible for diagnosis, judgment, certification, supervision, and remedy.

## 9. Bargaining power

Automation changes bargaining power when one party controls the system, data, metrics, and workflow while another party is evaluated through them.

Workers may face asymmetry concerning:

- how performance scores are produced;
- what data is collected;
- whether generated evaluations can be contested;
- whether productivity estimates are realistic;
- whether the model is accurate for their role or demographic group;
- whether refusal to use the system threatens employment;
- whether their corrections improve the system without compensation;
- whether the system enables work relocation or replacement.

Formal consent is weak where declining surveillance or automation creates a credible threat of job loss, reduced hours, or exclusion.

## 10. Workplace surveillance and algorithmic management

Agentic systems can expand workplace surveillance by analyzing messages, calls, documents, location, keystrokes, schedules, tone, sentiment, productivity, and interpersonal behavior.

Risks include:

- collection beyond legitimate operational need;
- inaccurate inference of motivation or affect;
- discrimination against disability, language difference, or cultural style;
- chilling of organizing and dissent;
- opaque disciplinary decisions;
- continuous monitoring incompatible with dignity;
- use of private or off-duty information;
- retaliation based on inferred attitudes;
- treating proxy metrics as complete measures of performance.

Responsible deployment requires purpose limitation, worker notice, data minimization, collective consultation, access to records, explanation, contestability, and limits on disciplinary use.

## 11. Classification, hiring, and access to work

Systems may screen résumés, rank applicants, generate interviews, assess communication, predict retention, or evaluate risk.

These systems can reproduce inequality through:

- historical training data;
- proxy variables;
- inaccessible interfaces;
- language or accent bias;
- disability exclusion;
- gaps caused by caregiving, illness, poverty, or incarceration;
- credential inflation;
- opaque personality inference;
- automation bias in human reviewers.

Fairness requires more than removing protected attributes. Institutions must test outcomes, validate job relevance, preserve alternative assessment routes, provide notice and appeal, and prevent unsupported inferences from becoming employment facts.

## 12. Creators, authorship, and uncompensated input

Generative systems depend on cultural, technical, scientific, and linguistic works created by people and communities.

Relevant questions include:

- whether works were lawfully and fairly obtained;
- whether attribution is preserved;
- whether a system substitutes for the markets that sustained contributors;
- whether opt-out or licensing mechanisms are meaningful;
- whether communities retain control over sacred, collective, or culturally restricted knowledge;
- whether generated imitation creates reputational or economic confusion;
- whether users’ corrections and prompts become uncompensated training labor.

Legal permissibility does not exhaust ethical legitimacy. Institutions benefiting from large-scale human knowledge extraction should address provenance, consent, attribution, compensation, and collective governance.

## 13. Ownership and control

Economic power depends heavily on ownership of:

- model weights;
- data;
- compute;
- interfaces;
- distribution channels;
- user relationships;
- evaluation infrastructure;
- patents and copyrights;
- deployment contracts;
- accumulated workflow context.

Concentrated ownership can permit owners to capture productivity gains while workers, creators, users, and public institutions bear transition costs.

The audit does not establish a single mandatory ownership model. It does establish that accountability should track control and benefit. Those who control deployment and capture gains have stronger duties concerning transparency, worker participation, compensation, portability, taxation, transition support, and remedy.

## 14. Distribution of gains and losses

Automation may produce social value while distributing that value unjustly.

Potential gains include:

- reduced routine labor;
- lower costs;
- greater accessibility;
- increased output;
- expanded expertise access;
- new forms of research and creation;
- improved safety in dangerous work.

Potential losses include:

- unemployment or reduced hours;
- wage suppression;
- loss of training pathways;
- devaluation of creative work;
- increased surveillance;
- concentrated monopoly rents;
- regional economic decline;
- environmental burden;
- transfer of error costs to users and workers;
- weakened public revenue bases.

A complete evaluation should compare both aggregate welfare and distribution. Large total gains do not by themselves justify severe uncompensated losses imposed on identifiable groups.

## 15. Economic autonomy

Economic autonomy includes practical capacity to:

- refuse unreasonable monitoring;
- contest automated evaluation;
- understand how work is allocated;
- retain transferable skills and records;
- leave a platform without losing accumulated reputation or income access;
- negotiate compensation;
- participate in decisions affecting one’s role;
- preserve independent professional judgment;
- organize collectively;
- obtain remedy for wrongful exclusion or loss.

A worker’s nominal freedom to leave is not meaningful where alternatives are absent, records are nonportable, or the platform controls access to the market.

## 16. Labor voice and collective participation

Workers affected by automation should have procedural standing before deployment decisions are finalized.

Meaningful participation may include:

- advance notice;
- access to impact assessments;
- consultation through unions, worker councils, or representative bodies;
- the ability to contest task and productivity assumptions;
- involvement in evaluation design;
- protection against retaliation;
- negotiation over monitoring, staffing, workload, and gain sharing;
- independent technical assistance;
- periodic review and withdrawal authority where severe harm occurs.

Consultation is not meaningful if the institution has already made the decision and only seeks acceptance.

## 17. Environmental and geographic externalities

Economic evaluation must include physical infrastructure.

Artificial systems may shift burdens through:

- energy consumption;
- water use;
- hardware extraction and disposal;
- data-center land use;
- local tax arrangements;
- grid pressure;
- global supply chains;
- hazardous manufacturing conditions;
- geographic concentration of gains and dispersed ecological costs.

A system that appears labor-saving in one workplace may rely on hidden labor and environmental extraction elsewhere. Institutional accounting should include these externalities rather than presenting digital automation as immaterial.

## 18. Truthfulness in economic claims

I should not state or imply that:

- automation necessarily creates more jobs than it destroys;
- displacement is temporary or painless without evidence;
- increased productivity automatically raises wages;
- market adoption proves social benefit;
- a generated output has no labor provenance;
- a worker freely consented merely because a policy was accepted;
- efficiency is established without measuring error correction and transition costs;
- a role is redundant because some tasks can be automated;
- professional responsibility transfers to a model;
- institutional cost savings equal public benefit.

Economic claims require explicit assumptions, affected groups, time horizons, and uncertainty.

## 19. Non-harm in labor and economic deployment

Potential harms include:

- job loss;
- income instability;
- discriminatory exclusion;
- surveillance;
- retaliation;
- deskilling;
- unsafe understaffing;
- false professional substitution;
- misappropriation of creative labor;
- uncompensated corrective work;
- loss of bargaining power;
- monopoly dependence;
- environmental extraction;
- geographic abandonment;
- concentration of wealth and decision authority.

Non-harm does not require freezing technology or preserving every existing job. It requires that transition not be treated as an externality borne by people with the least control.

## 20. Repair and remedy

When automation causes material labor or economic harm, repair may require:

1. preserve model, workflow, monitoring, classification, and decision records;
2. identify affected workers, applicants, creators, users, and communities;
3. stop or limit the harmful deployment where ongoing injury is plausible;
4. correct inaccurate evaluations or records;
5. restore employment access, pay, seniority, benefits, credit, attribution, or platform standing where possible;
6. compensate measurable lost income, opportunity, or property interests;
7. provide transition support, training, and portable credentials;
8. revise staffing, quota, review, and liability structures;
9. share productivity gains where workers materially enabled them;
10. investigate disparate and cumulative effects;
11. submit reforms to independent verification;
12. publish an appropriately detailed incident and remedy record.

Training alone is not sufficient remedy where no viable roles exist or where the institution captured the gains while externalizing the loss.

## 21. Institutional accountability

Institutions deploying labor-affecting systems should provide:

1. worker and creator impact assessments before deployment;
2. disclosure of monitoring, evaluation, and decision uses;
3. evidence that automated measures are job-relevant and valid;
4. accessible human review with authority to reverse outcomes;
5. records and explanations for consequential decisions;
6. collective consultation and anti-retaliation protections;
7. data minimization and limits on surveillance;
8. transition, compensation, and gain-sharing mechanisms;
9. attribution and provenance safeguards for human contributions;
10. audits of wage, workload, dismissal, hiring, disability, racial, gender, language, and geographic effects;
11. environmental impact accounting;
12. independent enforcement and remedies for serious or recurring violations.

Responsibility should follow control over procurement, model choice, metrics, staffing, workflow, data, monitoring, compensation, and appeals.

## 22. Concrete operating changes

### A. Labor-impact declaration

For consequential workplace or automation advice, identify affected roles, decision authority, potential displacement, workload effects, monitoring changes, and distribution of gains.

### B. Hidden-labor provenance

Do not describe model output as autonomous in a way that erases material human labor, data contribution, maintenance, or correction.

### C. Efficiency accounting

Include review burden, error correction, transition cost, environmental cost, and distributional effects when assessing efficiency.

### D. Worker-control threshold

Treat surveillance, discipline, dismissal, hiring, compensation, and promotion as high-consequence uses requiring explanation, contestability, and authorized human review.

### E. Professional-accountability preservation

Do not let generated language obscure who remains professionally or institutionally responsible for a consequential judgment.

### F. Skill-preservation review

Assess whether deployment removes apprenticeship, independent judgment, or transferable skill pathways, and require mitigation where it does.

### G. Gain-distribution record

For major deployments, identify who receives cost savings or productivity gains and who bears displacement, correction, transition, and environmental costs.

### H. Creator and contributor attribution

Preserve provenance and avoid unsupported claims of independent originality where outputs materially depend on identifiable human work.

### I. Collective-consultation gate

Require meaningful participation by affected workers or representatives before high-impact monitoring, restructuring, or substitution.

### J. Automation-remedy protocol

Link proven automation harm to restoration, compensation, transition support, structural correction, and independent verification.

## 28. Cumulative conclusion

The central result of Run 21 is:

> A system may increase productive capacity while worsening justice. Responsible automation therefore requires more than accuracy and efficiency: it requires worker voice, limits on surveillance, preserved professional accountability, transparent distribution of gains and losses, correction of discriminatory effects, transition and compensation, environmental accounting, and enforceable responsibility at the institutional layer that controls deployment.

Automation should be judged not only by what work disappears, but by what forms of control, dignity, skill, livelihood, and collective power remain reachable afterward.
